Build Your Family Agreement

A guided worksheet to fill in together — one adult, one young person, side by side. About 15–20 minutes.

Before you start

Who's making this agreement?

This works best out loud, together. One person can type while you both talk it through. You can stop and come back — your answers save as you go.

The most protective thing research points to isn't a longer list of rules or a warmer heart — it's both at once. This worksheet helps you write down the rules and the reasons, together.

Step 1 of 6

What we care about

Start here, not with the rules. Tap the ones that fit your family, add your own, then finish the sentence below.

When a young person understands the why, the rules stop feeling random — and they're far harder to rebel against than a rule that's just "because I said so."

Step 2 of 6

What that looks like day to day

Turn those values into everyday expectations — broad and positive. Tap any that fit, and add your own.

These aren't punishments-in-waiting. They're the ordinary habits that keep a family connected and safe.

Step 3 of 6

The rules

Every family sets its own rules. But these safety lines are the floor — read each one out loud and talk about the why.

Curfew, screens, check-ins, where you can go. This is the part you negotiate — the young person gets a real say here.

Step 4 of 6

What happens if a line is crossed

Agree on these now — calm, together — so no one is surprised later. Write them as "if this, then that."

Consequences work best when they're predictable, related to what happened, proportionate, and delivered calmly — then followed by repair. Consistency matters more than harshness. A consequence is meant to teach, not to make someone suffer.

Step 5 of 6

The one rule above all the others

The Safety Override

"If you're ever unsafe, call. You will not be in trouble for calling."

A young person who fears the consequence more than the danger will stay in the danger. This rule outranks every other one on the page. Anything else gets talked about later — once everyone is safe and calm.

Step 6 of 6

Catching it right

Rules get the attention, but noticing good choices shapes behavior just as powerfully — and most families do it too little.

Be specific. Instead of "good job," describe what you saw: "You texted me when your plans changed — that's why I trust you with a later curfew." And let trust buy freedom: responsibility shown → independence extended.
